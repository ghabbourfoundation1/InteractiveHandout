01

Atoms and materials

Key points
  • Nucleus: protons (+) and neutrons (neutral)
  • Electrons (−) orbit the nucleus
  • Protons = electrons → neutral atom
  • Same charges repel · opposite charges attract
  • The outer (valence) electrons decide the material
  • Electricity = electrons flowing from atom to atom
Handout text
The atom
  • Any material is made of atoms: a dense nucleus plus energy levels (orbits)
  • Nucleus: protons (positive) and neutrons (electrically neutral)
  • Energy levels: electrons (negative) orbit the nucleus
  • Protons = electrons, so the atom is neutral
  • Copper: 29 protons + 35 neutrons
Charges and electron flow
  • Same charges repel, different charges attract
  • Electrons are held by the protons’ opposite charge and keep moving in orbit because they repel each other
  • Electricity = electrons flowing from one atom to another
  • An electron that leaves an atom leaves a hole, so the atom becomes positive
  • The electron moves because the neighbouring atom looks more positive
  • The energy released when an electron jumps into another orbit is electrical energy
  • The number of electrons in the last orbit decides the material and how it conducts
Conductors
  • Fewer than 4 outer electrons (they lose electrons easily)
  • Copper: only one outer electron, easily knocked out to nearby atoms
  • Silver, gold, aluminum, steel, cast iron
Semiconductors
  • Exactly 4 outer electrons
  • Silicon, germanium, carbon
  • Used in transistors, computers and other electronic devices
Insulators
  • More than 4 outer electrons: easier to gain electrons than to release them
  • Rubber, plastic, nylon, porcelain, fiberglass
  • Ceramics (spark plugs)
  • Varnish (covers copper wires in alternators and starters)

Current direction

Key points
  • Electron theory: − → +
  • Conventional theory: + → −
  • Automotive diagrams and schematics use conventional theory
  • Current needs a closed loop
Handout text
Electricity
  • A form of energy, like heat, light and mechanical energy
  • The movement of electrons from one atom to another
Electron theory
  • Electricity moves from negative to positive
Conventional theory
  • Electricity moves from positive to negative
  • Automotive electricity uses conventional theory in all electrical diagrams and schematics
03

Voltage, current, resistance

Ohm’s law

Current

Voltage

Key points
  • Current I: charge per second, ampere (A)I = Q ÷ t
  • Voltage V: energy per coulomb, volt (V)V = E ÷ Q
  • Resistance R: opposition to current, ohm (Ω)
  • Ohm’s lawV = I × R
  • Voltage + a closed loop are needed to get current
  • Book: 12 V on 3 Ω → 4 A
Handout text
Current intensity (I)
  • Quantity of charge passing through a conductor per unit time
  • Unit: ampere (A) · Q in coulombs (C) · t in seconds
  • I = Q ÷ t
Voltage difference (V)
  • The work required to move a unit of charge between two points
  • Unit: volt (V) · E in joules (J)
  • V = E ÷ Q
Voltage vs current
  • Water analogy: voltage = pressure, current = flow
  • Current needs a voltage and a closed loop
Resistance (R)
  • Opposition to current flow, measured in ohms (Ω)
  • The ability to slow down the electrons
Ohm’s law
  • A current I through a resistance R creates a voltage V across it
  • V = I × R
  • I = V ÷ R · R = V ÷ I
  • Example: 12 V across 3 Ω → I = 12 ÷ 3 = 4 A
04

Power and energy

Power law: P = V × I

Now add resistance: I = V ÷ R

Lamp on the mains

Energy used

Key points
  • Power: the rate of doing work, watt (W)P = V × I
  • Energy: power × time, kilowatt-hour (kWh)E = P × t
  • Book: 100 W lamp on 220 V → 0.45 A
  • Book: 2000 W hot plate for 90 min → 3 kWh
Handout text
Energy and power
  • Energy: the capacity to do work (Wh, kWh)
  • Power: the rate at which work is done or energy is transmitted (W, kW)
  • Water analogy: power = flow rate, energy = amount of water in the bucket
Power
  • Product of voltage and current
  • P (W) = I (A) × V (V)
  • Example: 100 W lamp on 220 V → I = 100 ÷ 220 = 0.45 A
Energy transferred
  • Energy transferred in a circuit over a given time
  • E (kWh) = P (kW) × t (h)
  • Example: 2000 W hot plate for 90 min → 2 kW × 1.5 h = 3 kWh

Variable resistors

Photo resistors

Reading the bands
  • Digits first, then the number of zeros, then the tolerance

Tolerance
  • Silver ±10% · gold ±5% · brown ±1% · green ±0.5% · violet ±0.1%
Examples

Resistors in series

Key points
  • Constant current
  • Variable voltage (it is shared)
  • Resistances add upReq = R₁ + R₂ + … + Rₙ
  • Book: 3 kΩ + 10 kΩ + 5 kΩ = 18 kΩ
  • Book: 400 Ω + 200 Ω + 1 kΩ = 1600 Ω
Handout text
Series connection
  • Constant current, variable voltage
  • Req = R₁ + R₂ + … + Rₙ
Examples
  • 3 kΩ + 10 kΩ + 5 kΩ = 18 kΩ
  • 400 Ω + 200 Ω + 1 kΩ (1×10³) = 1600 Ω
08

Resistors in parallel

Key points
  • Constant voltage
  • Variable current (it is shared)
  • Add the reciprocals1/Req = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + … + 1/Rₙ
  • Book: 400 Ω, 200 Ω, 1 kΩ → 117.64 Ω
Handout text
Parallel connection
  • Constant voltage, variable current
  • Each branch: I = V ÷ R
  • 1/Req = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + … + 1/Rₙ
Example
  • 400 Ω, 200 Ω and 1 kΩ in parallel → Req = 117.64 Ω

Capacitors

Charge stored

Charging and discharging, in slow motion

In parallel and in series

Key points
  • Stores electric charge: two plates + a dielectric
  • Dielectric (an insulator that stores charge): glass, ceramic, plastic film, air, paper
  • Farad (F): µF = 10⁻⁶ · nF = 10⁻⁹ · pF = 10⁻¹²Q = C × V
  • Charging: current flows, charge builds up on the plates
  • Discharging: the capacitor acts as a source until its voltage is zero
  • Parallel: C = C₁ + C₂ + …Series: 1/C = 1/C₁ + 1/C₂ + …
Handout text
Definition and construction
  • A device that stores electric charge
  • Two separated metallic conductors (plates) with a dielectric in between
Dielectric
  • An insulator that can store charges
  • Glass, ceramic, plastic film, air, paper
  • It increases the capacitor’s charge capacity
Types
  • Polarized · non-polarized · variable polarized · variable non-polarized
Capacitance
  • Capacity to store charge for a given voltage; Q is proportional to V
  • Q = C × V
  • Unit: farad (F), typically pF to mF
  • µF = 10⁻⁶ F · nF = 10⁻⁹ F · pF = 10⁻¹² F
Charging
  • Initial charge is zero
  • Connected to a battery, current flows and charge builds up on the plates
Discharging
  • Battery removed, a load connected in series
  • The capacitor acts as a voltage source; current flows until its voltage is zero
Parallel and series
  • The opposite of resistors
  • Parallel: C = C₁ + C₂ + … + CₙSeries: 1/C = 1/C₁ + 1/C₂ + … + 1/Cₙ

Diodes

LED

SymbolLED (light emitting diode)
Key points
  • Conducts in one direction only: anode (+) → cathode (−)
  • Forward bias (above ≈ 0.7 V): closed switch
  • Reverse bias: open switch
  • Zener: voltage regulator
  • LED: emits light · long lead = anode (+)
  • Photodiode
Handout text
Diode
  • Two-terminal component that conducts current in one direction only
  • Ideal diode: zero resistance one way, infinite resistance the other way
  • Terminals: anode (+), cathode (−)
Forward vs reverse bias
  • Forward bias: switch closed, drop ≈ 0.7 V
  • Reverse bias: switch open
  • Too much reverse voltage causes breakdown
Types
  • Zener diode: voltage regulator
  • LED: semiconductor that emits light when current flows; long lead = anode (+), short lead = cathode (−)
  • Photodiode

Transistor

Handout text
Induction

Transformers

  • Step-up: primary turns fewer than secondary (N₁ < N₂)
  • Step-down: primary turns more than secondary (N₁ > N₂)
  • U₁ : U₂ = N₁ : N₂

Q2. Why do the negative electrons continue to orbit around the positive protons?

Same charges repel and different charges attract. They are attracted and held by the opposite charge of the protons, and they keep moving in orbit because they repel each other.

Q3. What is a transistor?

A semiconductor device used to amplify or switch electrical signals and power.

Q4. What is the definition of a dielectric?

An insulator that can store charges.

Q5. What are the types and symbols of capacitors?

Polarized, non-polarized, variable polarized and variable non-polarized (see chapter 10).
